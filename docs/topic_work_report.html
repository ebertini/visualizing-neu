Reading 2,741 research grants by what they mean

We replaced the keyword-counting model behind our topic analysis with one that reads each grant's language semantically. It sorts the portfolio into 25 research themes, groups those into 8 families, and — unlike the old model — is allowed to say “this one doesn't fit anywhere.”

The problem

The old model counted words. It couldn't tell a coastline from a cell.

Until now the project's topics came from LDA — a method that treats each abstract as a bag of words and looks for words that co-occur. It is fast and it was a reasonable starting point, but it has two failure modes that were doing real damage to our conclusions.

It confuses vocabularies. Two fields that borrow each other's words get merged, even when the research has nothing in common. And it never says “I don't know.” LDA assigns every document to its best-scoring topic, so a grant with a two-line title gets filed with the same apparent confidence as a grant with a 400-word abstract. On a chart, those look identical.

There was also a housekeeping problem: two separate topic models had grown up in parallel — one inside the analysis notebooks, another inside the interactive web visualisations — with different settings, different text cleaning, and different numbers of topics. Any figure quoted from one did not match the other.

The clearest symptom: Professor Alshawabkeh's environmental grants — soil and groundwater contamination in Puerto Rico — were being filed under Biomedical, because they share clinical vocabulary about exposure and health outcomes.

Old model · LDA
  • Puerto Rico contamination work → Biomedical
  • Every document forced into a topic
  • Two competing models (8 topics vs 12 topics)
  • Topic numbers reshuffle on every re-run, silently invalidating hand-written labels
New model · SPECTER2 + BERTopic
  • Same work → Structural engineering, Coastal & flood, Environmental health
  • 28.3% left explicitly Unassigned
  • One model, one label file, one text cleaner
  • Identical topic count across all tested random seeds

How the new method works, briefly. Each grant is passed through SPECTER2, a language model trained on scientific papers, which converts the text into a list of numbers capturing its meaning. Grants about similar science end up near each other in that space regardless of shared jargon. A clustering step then finds the dense regions — those become the themes — and leaves genuinely isolated documents unclustered rather than forcing them somewhere. Finally, the distinguishing words of each cluster are extracted to describe it.

What was built · four milestones, in order

From four scattered pipelines to one

M1

One text cleaner, one embedding path

Grant abstracts arrive full of funding-agency boilerplate, broken HTML markup, and stub records like 9501172 Kaeli, David. Two different cleaners existed and disagreed. They were merged into a single module with two settings, and locked down with a regression test suite built from five historical offenders — so the cleaning can never silently drift again.

M2

Recovering abstracts that had lost their grant

The source data contains a pile of abstracts that never matched a grant record. 403 of them had usable text. Each was traced back to a faculty member through a strict identity bridge, then fuzzy-matched against that person's grants on title, date and amount.

8 filled in a grant that previously had no abstract. 65 resolved to a person but no specific grant, and were kept as extra documents for the model. 19 turned out to be re-uploads of grants that already had abstracts — the plan as written would have counted those twice, so a duplicate check was added. The remaining 311 could not be attributed confidently and were dropped rather than guessed.

Choosing the number of themes by experiment, not by eye

The clustering step has one consequential setting: the smallest group of grants allowed to count as a theme. Five values were swept across three random seeds each, and the results compared on topic count, stability, how much falls out as unassigned, and how tight the resulting clusters are. Table below.

Each row is one setting, averaged over three random seeds. We wanted the most themes we could get while still producing the same answer every time, without one giant catch-all cluster swallowing the portfolio.

The bottom row is the cautionary one: allowing only large clusters looks better on the “unassigned” column, but one seed collapsed the entire portfolio into two topics with a single 2,610-document blob — and the ±6.2 spread means it would have told a different story on a different day. The chosen setting returned exactly 25 themes on every seed, with the largest holding under 10% of the corpus.

The result

25 themes, in 8 families

Themes were named by hand after reading each cluster's distinguishing terms and a sample of its grants; the families were then derived by clustering the themes themselves. Bars show how many documents sit in each theme.

One theme (#11) is marked as an artifact rather than a research area: it is largely placeholder records whose title is literally the word “Grant”. Naming it honestly is more useful than dressing it up as a field of study.

What this analysis does not claim

01
The $2.18B portfolio total is not money Northeastern raised. The source attributes a grant to a faculty member even when the award predates their NEU hire. Roughly $685M was earned at a prior institution. Any funding claim must state which basis it uses.
02
Only 72% of grants have usable abstract text. The rest are clustered on their titles alone. We checked that this doesn't quietly break things — title-only grants land in the unassigned bucket at 27.6% versus 28.0% for grants with abstracts — but they carry less signal.
03
NIH abstracts effectively stop after 2020. This is a collection gap in our source, not a funding decline. Any topic trend crossing 2020 will understate NIH-funded work until we backfill from NIH's own database.
04
The data is NSF/NIH-heavy — about 88% of dollars. Industry, foundation and internal funding are largely invisible here, so the themes describe federally-funded research, not all research.
05
28.3% unassigned is a feature, but it is still 28.3%. Those documents are genuinely heterogeneous — one-off awards, thin metadata, small interdisciplinary work. They are shown, not hidden, and they should not be silently excluded from denominators.

Reproducing this run. Every step is scripted and runs on a laptop CPU; the language model step needs network access the first time and takes about 7 minutes.

# from the repository root
python -m src.build_dataset             # raw spreadsheets → clean tables
python -m src.reconcile_orphans         # recover unmatched abstracts
python -m src.build_specter2_embeddings # semantic encoding  (local only)
python -m src.topics_bertopic           # fit + save the 25-theme model
python -m src.build_viz_data            # data for the interactive maps
pytest tests/test_clean_text.py         # cleaning regression tests
